<div className="deep-pane-header"><span className="pill purple">How it works</span><h2>The system mechanics</h2></div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Purpose
The Contract is the durable, human-owned definition of **what success means** for a bounded loop of work. Planning determines the route, Execution performs the work, and Validation judges the evidence—but none of those stages may quietly redefine the target.

A useful Contract is compact enough to reason against and strong enough to prevent three common failures: building the wrong thing quickly, expanding scope invisibly, and allowing implementation convenience to redefine acceptance.

<div className="deep-note deep-good"><strong>Core model:</strong> Contract = <strong>Goal / Spec / Proof</strong>. Loop Context sits immediately before it and tells the system what kind of lifecycle situation and work category it is dealing with.</div>
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### How it works

#### 0. Establish Loop Context
Before writing the Contract, classify enough context to choose appropriate planning and control strength:
- Product / System
- Lifecycle Context — Inception/Architecture, Build/Initial Delivery, Evolution, Operate/Sustain, Modernize/Migrate, or Retire
- Plan Category
- Scope boundary
- Current architecture version
- Risk / criticality
- Trigger or source of the work

Loop Context is metadata about the work. It is **not** part of Goal / Spec / Proof and does not become an excuse to create a giant intake form.

#### 1. Goal — define the outcome
Goal states the desired result in outcome language. It should be specific enough that a reviewer can tell whether the work mattered, but not so implementation-specific that it prematurely chooses the solution.

#### 2. Spec — define meaning and boundaries
Spec explains what the Goal means in this increment. It can include:
- functional requirements and behavior;
- constraints and policies;
- explicit non-goals;
- invariants that must remain true;
- assumptions and environmental boundaries;
- compatibility, security, performance, accessibility, or operational expectations.

**Non-goals are first-class.** They are one of the cheapest ways to stop agent-driven scope expansion.

#### 3. Proof — define required evidence
Proof states the evidence, checks, tests, reviews, observations, or acceptance demonstrations that must exist before the Goal and Spec may be considered satisfied.

<div className="deep-note"><strong>Proof is not a test plan.</strong> Contract Proof says <em>what evidence must exist</em>. Planning later decides <em>how that evidence will be produced</em>, including verification strategy, test assets, reviewers, environments, instrumentation, and thresholds.</div>

#### 4. Approve and lock
Once approved, the Contract becomes a controlled object. It may be superseded by an approved version, but an agent may not silently rewrite the active Contract during Planning, Execution, or Validation.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Inputs and outputs

<div className="deep-mini-flow">
<div><strong>Inputs</strong>User or mission need, observed problem, product context, architecture context, policies, constraints, evidence, prior decisions.</div>
<div><strong>Transformation</strong>Human + AI refine intent into Goal / Spec / Proof and expose ambiguity, conflicts, non-goals, and acceptance evidence.</div>
<div><strong>Outputs</strong>Approved versioned Contract, Loop Context metadata, source/provenance references, and any unresolved assumptions or explicit risks.</div>
</div>

A Contract should be addressable by stable identity/version so Plans, tasks, evidence, Validation results, Decision Records, and change proposals can trace back to the exact approved target.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Interactions with the rest of AE
- **Planning** consumes the Contract and must produce a route that satisfies it. Planning may propose a Contract change; it cannot mutate the Contract.
- **Architecture** constrains feasible interpretations of the Spec and gives Planning a real system model to reason against.
- **Execution** adapts locally inside the reviewed Plan while preserving Contract boundaries.
- **Validation** independently compares produced evidence against Contract Proof.
- **Human Owner** owns consequential intent and accepts or rejects Contract versions and change proposals.
- **Knowledge / memory** preserves the approved Contract, its provenance, prior versions, and relevant downstream impacts.
- **Observability** may be part of the evidence path when Proof depends on runtime behavior.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Access, authority, and enforcement
The Contract is intentionally asymmetric: many actors may contribute information, but only authorized decision holders may change the accepted target.

**Decision Authority (DA):** the Human Owner retains authority to approve the Contract and any later change. Organizations may define additional required approvers for regulated, safety-critical, security-sensitive, financial, or mission-significant work.

**Operational Authority (OA):** agents may read the Contract, reference it, annotate working state, and create a **versioned change proposal**. They do not receive OA to overwrite the approved Contract object.

**Enforcement:** the system should fail closed on unauthorized mutation attempts and retain an auditable version transition when an approved proposal creates a new Contract version.

```text
contract_mutable_by_agents: false
changes_via_versioned_proposal_only: true
any_change_requires_owner_approval: true
invite_change_proposals: true
audit_all_version_transitions: true
downstream_impact_review: true
```

The intent is not to reject new information. It is to **welcome new information; control mutation**.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Change proposal anatomy
A strong proposal carries enough information for a human to make a decision without rediscovering the entire situation:
1. trigger / new evidence;
2. rationale;
3. exact proposed diff;
4. alternatives considered;
5. downstream impact on Plan, architecture, tasks, evidence, cost, schedule, or risk;
6. consequence if deferred;
7. priority recommendation;
8. provenance / source pointers.

Approved changes create a new version. Rejected proposals remain evidence that the issue was considered.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Human knowledge, skills, and abilities
The Contract requires more than approval authority. Human contributors bring:
- contextual knowledge of users, mission, organization, policy, and history;
- logic to distinguish outcome from implementation detail;
- ingenuity and creativity when reframing the problem;
- judgment about meaningful tradeoffs and acceptable evidence;
- values and risk appetite;
- authority and accountability for consequential commitments.

A weak Human Owner who simply accepts machine-generated wording creates a nominal control, not meaningful governance.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Agent support
AI is useful as a **Contract facilitator**, not Contract owner. It can:
- interview for missing intent;
- detect ambiguous words and hidden assumptions;
- distinguish Goal from solution detail;
- surface likely non-goals and preservation constraints;
- challenge vague Proof such as “works correctly”;
- inspect architecture or existing product behavior to expose conflicts;
- propose exact version diffs when new information appears;
- trace downstream artifacts potentially affected by a proposed change.

The agent should communicate with human-native artifacts—conversation, examples, diagrams, alternatives, consequences—not force the human to fill out a form merely because the machine prefers fields.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Failure modes
- **Vague Goal:** “Improve the app” gives Planning no durable outcome.
- **Spec as giant requirements dump:** useful meaning is buried in low-signal text.
- **Solution masquerading as Goal:** the Contract prematurely fixes architecture or implementation.
- **No non-goals:** agents expand into adjacent “helpful” work.
- **Proof = “tests pass”:** evidence is underspecified and implementation gets to define success.
- **Mutable acceptance criteria during execution:** the doer changes the judge’s target.
- **Frozen against reality:** teams refuse legitimate change because “the Contract is immutable.” Immutability applies to unauthorized mutation, not to approved version evolution.
- **Approval theater:** a human clicks approve without understanding the consequential choices.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Implementation options
The architecture does not require one storage product. A Contract can live in:
- a versioned repository document plus machine-readable companion;
- a database-backed portal object;
- a work-management system with immutable approved versions;
- a signed artifact in a regulated workflow;
- a hybrid system where the human-readable view is rendered from structured canonical data.

Minimum capabilities matter more than vendor choice: stable identity, version history, explicit approval, protected mutation, provenance, traceability, and downstream impact visibility.
</div>

<section className="deep-pane deep-work">
<div className="deep-pane-header"><span className="pill blue">How we work</span><h2>Human Owner + AI practice</h2></div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Purpose in practice
Treat Contract formation as a short reasoning conversation, not a document-production ceremony. The objective is shared understanding strong enough to let agents do substantial downstream work without continually guessing what “done” means.

A good session feels closer to two colleagues at a whiteboard: the AI asks, sketches, challenges, and summarizes; the Human Owner corrects context and makes consequential choices.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Step 1 — orient before asking for answers
The AI should first gather the smallest useful context: what product/system this is, why the work exists, lifecycle state, relevant architecture, current behavior, and known constraints.

The Human Owner should correct false premises early. A five-minute correction here can avoid hours of excellent execution against the wrong problem.

**Useful AI questions:**
- What observable outcome would make this worth doing?
- Who experiences the result?
- What existing behavior must not change?
- What are we explicitly not trying to solve in this increment?
- Which constraints are real policy/mission constraints versus inherited assumptions?
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Step 2 — draft Goal in outcome language
The AI proposes a concise Goal and at least one alternative framing when ambiguity is meaningful.

The Human Owner checks whether the wording describes the result rather than the implementation. If the Goal names a database, framework, agent count, or UI pattern, ask whether that is genuinely a required constraint or merely an early solution idea.

<div className="deep-note deep-good"><strong>Healthy signal:</strong> different technical Plans could satisfy the same Goal.</div>
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Step 3 — build Spec around decisions and boundaries
Work outward from the Goal. Capture only information that changes meaning, acceptance, risk, or feasible implementation.

Use concrete categories as prompts, not mandatory bureaucracy:
- behavior / user rules;
- preservation / invariants;
- security / privacy;
- performance / reliability;
- platform / environment;
- compatibility / integration;
- policy / regulatory constraints;
- non-goals;
- assumptions needing validation.

When a requirement conflicts with architecture or another constraint, the AI should surface the collision explicitly rather than smoothing it into prose.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Step 4 — write Proof as evidence
Ask: **What would we need to see to confidently accept this?**

Possible Proof can include:
- representative user scenarios;
- functional or security checks;
- benchmark thresholds;
- architecture or code review evidence;
- deployment and runtime observations;
- accessibility evaluation;
- telemetry showing operational behavior;
- human acceptance of a visual or mission outcome.

Do not over-specify the implementation of those checks here. Planning owns the verification/test strategy.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Step 5 — adversarial read before approval
Have the AI perform a fresh Contract review from several perspectives:
- **Planner:** can I derive a route without inventing requirements?
- **Executor:** where could I interpret this two different ways?
- **Validator:** can I tell what evidence is sufficient?
- **Risk reviewer:** which consequential constraint is missing?
- **User/stakeholder:** does the stated outcome match the actual need?

Resolve material ambiguity or mark it explicitly. Do not hide uncertainty simply to make the artifact look complete.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Step 6 — Human Owner approves
Approval should be an understanding gate. The interface should surface the exact Goal, important Spec decisions/non-goals, and Proof—not merely present a long generated document and an Approve button.

After approval, the system records the version and protects it from agent mutation.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### When new information arrives
Do not tell agents to ignore reality. Tell them to stop silent mutation.

**Working loop:**
1. Agent observes new evidence.
2. Agent decides whether it is local implementation detail, a Plan issue, or a Contract issue.
3. If Contract-level, agent drafts a versioned proposal with exact diff and impacts.
4. Human Owner accepts, rejects, or requests revision.
5. If accepted, downstream Plan / tasks / Proof strategy are reviewed for impact.
6. Work resumes against the new approved version.

This makes change easy to propose and impossible to hide.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Coaching the Human Owner
Early teams often need practice distinguishing:

| Weak framing | Better question |
| --- | --- |
| “Build a React app.” | What user/mission outcome requires an application, and is React actually constrained? |
| “Make it secure.” | Which identities, data, threats, and protected actions matter? |
| “All tests pass.” | What evidence demonstrates the required behaviors and risks? |
| “Also integrate Teams while you’re there.” | Is that required for this increment or a separate proposal? |
| “The agent found a better requirement.” | What evidence changed, what exact Contract diff is proposed, and who approves it? |

The goal is not perfect prose. The goal is disciplined intent.
</div>

<div className="deep-section">
### Practical completion checklist
Before handing to Planning, ask:
- Can someone explain the Goal in one sentence?
- Are important non-goals explicit?
- Are invariants/preservation requirements visible?
- Are hidden implementation choices separated from actual constraints?
- Does Proof describe evidence rather than merely “tests”?
- Is the Human Owner actually authorized to approve this target?
- Is the approved version protected and traceable?
- Would a planner know when to return with a Contract-change proposal instead of guessing?

If yes, stop elaborating. Heavier Contract process is not more mature.
</div>
